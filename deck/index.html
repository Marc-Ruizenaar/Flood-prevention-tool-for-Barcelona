The problem

Barcelona already knows where water fails. That knowledge sits in four separate atlases.

Sewer overflowStreets where drains overflow on a 10-year storm. BCASA / Resilience Atlas.
14 flood hotspotsNamed problem spots from the city's drainage plan. RESCCUE / PDISBA.
Buried streams and tanksOld streams under the avenues, and the tanks built to hold them.
Slopes and torrentsWater running off Collserola into hillside neighbourhoods. ICGC / INUNCAT.

Nobody looks at all four at once. After each storm, crews find the same gaps again.

Point at each tile. The punchline: no single view, so slope risk falls between the cracks.

Methodology

Three ways of knowing, one rule.

Published maps

What the city already knows. We turned the atlases into 33 places, each with a fact card and a source link.

Reviewed reports

What actually happened. City press releases and Betevé news, read by a person before anything reaches the map.

Physics model

Why water goes where it goes. A water-flow simulation on a 30 m terrain grid, fed by real rain gauges. It reads no reports.

Every claim ends in a source link. We never forecast millimetres, depths or damage.

Three independent legs. The model and the reports don't see each other, so when they agree it means something.

How a report reaches the map

Software finds it. A person approves it.

ReadCity press and Betevé feeds, twice an hour.
→
QueueItems with flood words go to a review list, busiest neighbourhood first.
→
A person checksOpens the link, confirms the date and place, picks what failed.
→
MapOne dot per neighbourhood, for 7 days, with the link.
  • Places come from a list of Barcelona's 73 neighbourhoods. Never from AI.
  • Posts by private people are never read or stored.
Why manual review: the feeds don't say where, and a wrong dot on a flood map is worse than no dot.

Blind test on 2024 and 2025 storms, 73 neighbourhoods, each year predicted only from earlier data. Score is AUC: bar length shows the distance above a coin flip.

Maps alone barely beat a coin flip, so we call the map a briefing, not a prediction. Online chatter knows when it rains (79% of storm days, 18% of dry days) but not where.

This is the slide that earns trust. We measured, it was humbling, and it shaped the product: cite, don't predict.

Strategy

Join what's published. Cite everything. Keep a person in the loop.

Join, don't predict

The data exists and the city paid for it. The gap is one view, built for crews.

Trust before reach

A source link on every card and a person on every report. Wrong dots cost more than missing ones.

Cheap to keep alive

A static site with no server, open data, and a review step that takes minutes a day.

October 2026 is our test, not our training data.

Who it's for: municipal staff and district crews preparing before rain, not residents during it.
