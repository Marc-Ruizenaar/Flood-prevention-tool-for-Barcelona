# How it works: the scan intro and the scenario model

Plain-English guide for presenting this branch (`feat/ml-scenarios-intro`) and answering
technical questions. Every number comes from `data/ml/metrics.json` (generated by `ml/run_all.py`).

## 1. The whole system in 5 sentences

1. A **physics model** (in `backend/`) simulated how water moved over Barcelona's streets on two
   real rainy days, 2 and 3 October 2026, and recorded how deep the water got on each of 2,224 street segments.
2. A small **machine-learning model** (in `ml/`) studied those results, together with public terrain data
   (height, slope, where water collects), to learn which streets tend to flood.
3. That model then answers **what-if questions**: "if 75 mm of rain fell and the drains cleared 10 mm per hour,
   which streets would be most at risk?" It scores 15 combinations (5 rain amounts x 3 drain levels).
4. The new **glass scan intro** shows one of those scenarios: a sweep over the map, pulsing alerts on the
   10 riskiest areas, a 0 to 10 city risk score, "where to go" advice, and the model's honest report card.
5. **Enter map** closes the intro and drops you into the existing detailed map, which is unchanged.

## 3. Physics model vs ML model, and why both

- **Physics model**: simulates water flowing over the ground, minute by minute. It is slow (hours per storm)
  and needs heavy setup, but it follows real physical rules.
- **ML model**: a fast copy ("surrogate") of the physics model. Once trained it scores a new scenario in
  a fraction of a second, which is what makes a what-if slider possible.
- The catch: the ML model can only be as good as what it copied, and it only had **2 storms** to copy from.

The model type is **gradient-boosted decision trees** (scikit-learn `HistGradientBoostingClassifier`).
It is a standard choice for table data: fast, works on a laptop, and tells you which inputs it relied on.

## 4. Where the labels come from, and their limits

- **Label** = "flooded" if the physics model put **15 cm or more** of water on that street segment.
- **Rows**: 2,224 streets x 2 storms = 4,448 rows. 40 rows were removed: streets near the edge of the
  simulated box on 3 October, where the box walls make water pile up unrealistically ("edge ponding").
- **2 October** was light (15.5 mm): only 1% of streets flooded. **3 October** was heavy (143 mm):
  about half of all streets flooded, which is suspiciously high and probably still includes some box-wall effect.
- **Independent check**: 10 real reported flood places inside the box (from RESCCUE, the sewer stress map
  and the October 2026 proof point). The model **never trained on these**.
- **Not obtained**: the official Catalan flood zones (MAPRI). The government map server blocks automated
  downloads (HTTP 403). We did not try to get around the block.

## 5. The real numbers

### The error matrix (spatial cross-validation, 4,408 rows)

Each street was scored by a model that **never saw its 500 m neighbourhood** during training,
so nearby near-copies cannot inflate the score.

|  | Physics says flooded | Physics says dry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Model says flooded** | 592 correct alarms | 599 false alarms |
| **Model says dry** | 493 missed floods | 2,724 correct "dry" |

In words:
- **Accuracy 75.2%.** But a lazy model that always says "dry" gets **75.4%**. So accuracy alone shows **no skill**.
- **Balanced accuracy 68.3%**: the average of "how many floods it caught" and "how many dry streets it got right".
  50% would be pure guessing.
- **Precision 49.7%**: when it says "flooded", it is right about half the time.
- **Recall 54.6%**: it catches about half of the floods.
- **AUC 0.83**: looks good, but **it is misleading**. 96% of the model's decisions come from one input:
  how much rain exceeded the drains. That input just tells the two storms apart.
  **Inside the heavy storm alone, its ranking of streets scores 0.51, which is chance.**
  Inside the light storm it scores 0.84, but that rests on only 24 flooded streets, so it is shaky.
- **Calibration**: the raw score was overconfident. When it said "80 to 100% chance", those streets actually
  flooded 48% of the time. We now correct it with an isotonic map (a monotone step curve) fitted on the
  neighbourhood-held-out scores, and test the fix honestly: for each of the 5 neighbourhood groups, the curve
  is fitted on the other 4 and applied to the held-out one. Brier score (lower is better) goes from 0.144 to 0.132.
  One rule: no step of the curve may rest on fewer than 30 streets, so it cannot say "100%" from a handful.

  | The model says | Streets | Simulation flooded (raw score) | Simulation flooded (corrected) |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | 60 to 80% | 4 (raw: 668) | 48% | 75% |
  | 80 to 100% | 0 (raw: 99) | 48% | none |

  The middle of the range is now honest (40 to 60% says 49%, flooded 49%). Two warnings. First, correcting the
  score cannot add skill: inside a heavy storm most streets land on about 50%, and only 4 streets get a
  corrected score above 60%, so that row is rough. Second, the highest level the curve can now give is 71%, so
  the model rarely says a high number. In the app, "medium" starts at 30% and "high" at 60%; alerts and
  "where to go" advice use the 30% line.

### The honest scenario test: leave one storm out

This is what happens when you ask about a storm the model has never seen.

| Trained on | Tested on | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light day (2 Oct) | Heavy day (3 Oct) | Caught **16 of 1,061** flooded streets (recall 1.5%). AUC 0.51, chance |
| Heavy day (3 Oct) | Light day (2 Oct) | Caught 22 of 24 floods, but raised **1,088 alarms** for them (precision 2%). AUC 0.74 |

Meaning: **it does not yet know how storm size changes which streets flood.**

### Real-world check (10 reported flood places)

| Within | Places near one of the model's top 10 areas | Random points would get | Lift |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 150 m | 0 of 10 | 6.7% | 0 |
| 300 m | 3 of 10 | 19.4% | 1.55 |

3 hits out of 10 is **too few to mean anything statistically**: pure luck would give 3 or more hits about
30% of the time. The random points also land in parks and at the sea edge, which flatters the lift a little.

### Trust level: LOW

The trust rules were written into `ml/config.py` **before** the results came in, so nobody can tune them afterwards.

| Rule | Needed | Got | Passed? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| High: unseen-storm AUC | 0.80 or more | 0.51 | No |
| High: real-world lift at 300 m | above 1.5 | 1.55 | Yes (but on only 10 places) |
| High: reported places | 20 or more | 10 | No |
| High: storms trained on | 5 or more | 2 | No |
| Medium: neighbourhood-held-out AUC | 0.75 or more | 0.83 | Yes, but only because it tells the storms apart. Kept as written because the rule was fixed in advance |
| Medium: unseen-storm AUC | 0.65 or more | 0.51 | No |
| Medium: storms trained on | 3 or more | 2 | No |

### How the scenario slider really behaves

Because it learned from just two storms, the model learned one on/off step, not a smooth curve:
- At 20 mm/h drains, everything from 25 to 100 mm looks like the light day (risk 0.2 of 10, no street at or above 50%).
  143 mm looks like the heavy day (risk 4.7 of 10). Its share of streets at or above 50% reads only 2%, because
  most streets sit at 49.9% after correction, just under the cut. That cut is fragile: a 49% cut would read 61%.
- The drain setting works only through a fixed formula (rain minus drains x 3 hours), not something learned.
  That is why every scenario with drains other than 20 mm/h is marked **Extrapolated** in the intro.
- The alert lists skip the 40 streets dropped from training as edge ponding (water piling against the
  simulation's closed walls), so the intro never pulses an alert on that artefact.

### Why terrain could not do better (checked, not a bug)

- The terrain values are sensible (heights 15 to 100 m).
- The physics depths have real street patterns: neighbouring streets have similar depths.
- But the physics model also uses roof cover, surface roughness and soil soaking, on a sharper official
  terrain map. Our ML model only sees the shape of a free public terrain map, so it misses what drives the pattern.

## 6. What would make it better, ranked

1. **More physics storms**: at least 5, of different sizes, with open box edges. This fixes the step and the edge ponding. Biggest single gain.
2. **Give the ML the physics model's own inputs** (its terrain map, roofs, roughness, soil). Likely fixes "terrain at chance".
3. **Real incident labels**: fire brigade (Bombers) and city police (Guardia Urbana) callouts, or the 112 emergency log, per street.
4. **BCASA's city flood map** as data, as a second independent label.
5. **Several drain levels in the simulation**, so drainage is learned, not assumed.
6. **Per-section sewer capacity**, instead of one city-wide drain rate.
7. **Simulate the whole city**, so the real-world test has 20+ places instead of 10.
8. **Sharper terrain** (ICGC 2 m).

## 8. Q&A cheat sheet

**Is this a weather prediction?**
No. It answers "if this much rain fell, which streets would be most exposed?" The rain amount is your input.

**How accurate is it?**
On streets it had not seen: 75% accuracy, but always guessing "dry" gets the same. It catches about half
the floods and half its alarms are right. On a storm it had not seen, it is close to guessing. Trust: low.

**Then why show it?**
To show the full pipeline working end to end, with honest testing built in. The app shows the trust level,
the reasons, and the error matrix on screen. The weak part is the training data (2 storms), and we know how to fix it.

**Why not deep learning?**
4,448 rows is far too little for deep learning. Gradient-boosted trees are the standard for small table data,
are fast, and show which inputs they used.

**How do you avoid overfitting / cheating on the test?**
Streets are tested by a model that never saw their 500 m neighbourhood. We also test on a whole unseen storm,
and on 10 real reported places the model never saw. The trust rules were fixed in code before the results.

**Why is AUC 0.83 not good news?**
Because it mostly measures "can it tell a light storm from a heavy one", which is easy. Inside one storm,
ranking streets is at 0.51, which is chance.

**What does "70%" mean?**
It means: of the streets the model scores like this one, about 70% flooded in our physics simulation for that rain.
Measured on streets the correction never saw, "about 68%" turned out to be 75% (4 streets), which is a very small sample, so
treat it as rough. It is not a 70% chance of a real flood, because we have not checked it against real floods yet.

**Is "80%" on a street an 80% chance?**
Not exactly. After correction the model almost never goes that high: the highest level is 71%, and in the test no street
reached 80%. Treat anything above 60% as "high", not as a precise figure.

**Where do the labels come from?**
From our own physics simulation, not from real floods. So the ML is a fast copy of the physics model,
and inherits its assumptions (like drains clearing 20 mm per hour).

**What is "Extrapolated"?**
A scenario outside what the model learned from: drain levels other than 20 mm/h, or rain outside 15.5 to 143.4 mm.
Read those with extra care.

**What is the 0 to 10 risk score?**
10 times the average corrected score over all 2,224 streets in that scenario.

**How does "where to go" work?**
For each risky area it looks for the nearest street within 600 m (straight line) that is higher, lower risk,
and below the model's own flood cut-off of 0.5
(if none, it says so plainly), and pairs it with
the matching official preventive action from `data/zones.json`. It is a pointer, not an evacuation route.

**What would it cost to make it trustworthy?**
Mainly compute time and data access: 5+ more physics runs with open edges, and real incident data from
the city (Bombers, Guardia Urbana, 112). No new model type is needed.

**Is any personal data used?**
No. Terrain tiles, physics output and published reports only.

**Does the intro change the existing map?**
No. It is a layer on top. Enter map closes it, and the Scan button reopens it.
